\section{Introduction}

Text embedding models have become a basic infrastructure layer for semantic search, clustering, reranking, and retrieval-augmented generation. Their usefulness comes not only from individual vectors, but from the geometric structure those vectors induce: neighborhoods, semantic regions, and smooth paths between related examples. Strong recent embedding models, including LLM-based encoders, often learn rich semantic geometry but are costly to run at scale. Encoding large private collections, refreshing indexes as documents change, or serving retrieval on constrained hardware therefore motivates compact student encoders that inherit the geometry of a stronger teacher.

Knowledge distillation offers a practical route to this compression~\citep{hinton2015distilling}. In language representation learning it has been applied through output matching, hidden-state alignment, self-attention transfer, and compact pretrained architectures~\citep{sanh2019distilbert,jiao2019tinybert,wang2020minilm,xu2024survey}. For embedding models the common objectives are pointwise alignment to teacher coordinates, contrastive learning with teacher-derived positives, teacher-anchored layer alignment, and relational losses that match pairwise similarities within a mini-batch. Each supervises at a different granularity: individual examples, internal layers, sampled pairs, or local batch structure.

The last of these is the closest to what retrieval actually needs, and it is where this paper starts. A mini-batch is a uniform sample of the corpus, but an anchor's semantic neighbors occupy a set of measure $k/N$ within it, so they are almost never present. The consequence is sharper than slow learning: at the objective's own optimum, the pairs a batch never contained are subject to no constraint whatsoever, so whatever similarity the student assigns them reflects its own inductive bias rather than the teacher's geometry. Local geometry is the part of the teacher a batch-relational objective transfers least, and closing the gap by training longer costs a factor of order $N/B$ in epochs.

The geometric view suggests where to look instead. A teacher embedding space can be treated as a sampled semantic manifold: nearby examples define local neighborhoods, and multi-hop paths reveal semantic continuity. If the teacher embeddings define a discrete manifold over training examples, random walks on it reveal how semantic mass flows from each anchor to nearby examples, broader regions, and higher-order communities. Matching that diffusion process gives the student geometry-aware supervision tied neither to teacher internals nor to a particular student architecture.

We propose \textbf{RIPPLE}, a manifold distillation method for compact text embedding models. RIPPLE caches teacher embeddings once, builds a mutual $k$-nearest-neighbor graph from teacher cosine similarity, and reads the graph as a discrete approximation of the teacher's semantic manifold. From its transition matrix it precomputes diffusion targets across a range of diffusion times, from the ambient teacher metric itself to one-step, two-step, and longer-range semantic random walks, and trains the student to reproduce them on small candidate sets drawn in proportion to diffusion mass and pooled across the mini-batch. A second term extends supervision past the anchors: random walks select non-anchor nodes the batch has already encoded, and the student reproduces the teacher's one-step transition row there as well, which both densifies supervision and pins down similarity offsets that anchor-only matching leaves free. After the graph is built the teacher is discarded, so training never runs a teacher forward pass.

Two design decisions keep this family from collapsing into target smoothing. Matching one-step diffusion is already enough to control every longer diffusion time, but only up to an error that grows with it, so the broader scales earn their place by removing that growth. And they only do so if the student answers each scale at its own resolution: with one shared student distribution the multi-scale objective is exactly a single-scale objective against the mixture. RIPPLE needs no pointwise regression, contrastive pair construction, layer alignment, or graph-coordinate objective, and adds no trainable parameters beyond the student encoder.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We show that a mini-batch relational objective identifies an anchor's similarities only on the pairs its batches happened to contain---a fraction of order $TB/N$ of its neighbors---so at any practical schedule it supplies no teacher signal on nearly all of the local geometry it is meant to transfer (Section~\ref{sec:theory}).
    \item We introduce RIPPLE, which distills a diffusion-time family of neighborhood distributions from a teacher-induced mutual $k$NN graph into a compact student encoder, and derive each of its components from the requirement it has to meet: why local matching controls global geometry, why broader scales still tighten it, why small sampled candidate sets suffice, why the scales must be matched at distinct resolutions, and why one calibration term completes the loss (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
    \item Across nine datasets and three teacher--student pairs, RIPPLE attains the best overall average among compact students in all three settings, with the largest gains on semantic textual similarity; ablations test each theoretical prediction separately (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).
\end{itemize}
