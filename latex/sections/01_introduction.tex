\section{Introduction}

Text embedding models support semantic search, clustering, reranking, and retrieval-augmented generation. Their utility depends not only on individual vectors but on the geometry those vectors induce: which examples are neighbors, how sharply they are separated, and how local neighborhoods connect. Strong embedding teachers often learn this structure at substantial inference cost. A compact student is useful only if it inherits that structure rather than merely reproducing isolated outputs.

Relational knowledge distillation appears to target this requirement directly: it matches teacher and student similarities among examples in a mini-batch~\citep{park2019rkd,passalis2020pkt}. The comparisons, however, are selected by the data loader rather than by the teacher. For a corpus of $N$ examples, an anchor sees only $B-1$ other examples per epoch, almost none of which are among its teacher-defined semantic neighbors. After $T$ epochs, only an $O(TB/N)$ fraction of those neighbors has ever co-occurred with the anchor. The problem is not merely that the resulting estimate is noisy. Similarities to examples that never co-occur do not appear in the empirical objective at all, so the objective does not directly identify the local teacher relations it is meant to transfer.

We propose \textbf{RIPPLE}, a \emph{corpus-anchored geometry-distillation} method. RIPPLE first caches the teacher embeddings and constructs a mutual $k$-nearest-neighbor transition operator over the entire distillation corpus. Training comparisons are then selected from this teacher operator, not inherited accidentally from a mini-batch. The student matches the teacher's one-step neighborhood distribution and a small family of longer diffusion distributions on sampled candidate sets. This turns diffusion into a transfer mechanism: accurate one-step matching controls the error of every longer walk, while direct supervision at broader times tightens the resulting bound.

Two additional pieces make operator matching practical and coherent. First, diffusion-weighted sampling covers most of a target row with a candidate budget governed by the target's concentration rather than by the corpus size; candidates from all anchors are pooled and encoded once per batch. Second, row-wise softmax matching identifies similarity differences within a row but is invariant to an additive row offset in score space. RIPPLE therefore uses teacher random walks to select additional rows for supervision. Reciprocal overlaps between neighboring supervised rows, together with the symmetry of cosine similarity, encourage their offsets to agree and make the learned rows more consistent across anchors. The teacher is discarded after preprocessing, and student training uses no teacher hidden states, online teacher passes, or task labels.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formalize the coverage and objective-level identification limits of mini-batch relational distillation: after $T$ epochs, an anchor has been compared with at most an $O(TB/N)$ fraction of its teacher neighbors, and unseen relations are absent from the empirical score-level objective (Section~\ref{sec:theory}).
    \item We introduce RIPPLE, which distills a corpus-level teacher neighborhood operator through one-step and multi-scale diffusion targets, efficient mass-preserving candidate sampling, and overlapping-row supervision for cross-anchor consistency (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
    \item Across nine datasets and three teacher--student pairs, RIPPLE obtains the best mean overall score among the evaluated compact students in all three settings, with the clearest gains on semantic textual similarity. Controlled studies are designed to test graph breadth, multi-scale supervision, row supervision, and sampling efficiency separately (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).
\end{itemize}
